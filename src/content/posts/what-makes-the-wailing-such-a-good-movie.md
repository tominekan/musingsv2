A few weeks ago, I had the pleasure of watching the Korean horror movie “The Wailing” with a few of my friends. I haven’t been able to get this movie out of my head not gonna lie. As a quick side note, I’m never able to remember character names in movies or books, so I’ll identify them by how they looked to me.

**[SPOILERS AHEAD]**

The story is centered around a detective who’s investigating a series of unusual murders. He’s pretty skeptical about the supernatural nature of these cases until he starts getting dreams of being attacked by a demon, and his daughter starts behaving oddly. His life soon spirals into a mess of confusion, panic, and, ultimately tragedy. The movie was amazing overall, I’m going to note a few things that really stood out to me.


### The Good vs Evil trope is completely tossed aside

In most horror movies, there’s a very clearly malevolent force that characters have to figure out how to defeat. Not in this movie.

Throughout the story, you’re left guessing who’s good and evil, and never really sure who’s in the right. I think he achieves this effect by using subtle misdirections. For example, the old man often portrayed as the evil spirit, and the fact that he’s a Japanese foreigner in this remote Korean village warns us that he might not have come for good intentions (or it might be a comment on xenophobia). His shack in the woods is full od dead crows and heavy demonic imagery. What’s worse, he has a little cubby with pictures of all the town’s victims. Interestingly, his discheveled look can signal a lot of things, either marking his struggle against this powerful evil force, or a demon slowly corrupting it’s human flesh. The fact that he remains mute for most of the story doesn’t help with his image.

Next you have the shaman, whose looks initially don’t really convince us that he’s a good guy. He’s got bling all over him, and charges a hefty sum for his services.  This doesn’t really fit the look of a monk/shaman (I’m probably conflating these two here) who’s supposed give up all attachments to the material world. However, he pretty quickly finds the dead bird (evil presence) inside the jar of soy sauce and temporarily helps the daughter get over her posession. In addition, as he’s performing the ritual, we see back and forth scenes, detailing the spiritual battle between the shaman and the demon, and he nearly kills the demon, which in my eyes, planted him solidly as the good guy.

Lastly, we’re introduced to a beautiful but eccentric lady in white (subtle signaling that she’s “good“) making lackluster efforts to help the officer. Later in the story, she attacks the shaman, who then frantically calls the officer, claiming that he’s made a mistake in his reading and warns that’s she’s truly the evil spirit here. And honestly, her refusal to clearly explain herself (and her motivations) doesn’t really help with determining her alignment in the story.

### The characters actions (largely) make sense 

Another staple in horror movies is that main characters do stupid stuff that real people would (or really should) NEVER do. In my opinion, it makes the watching experience really bad not going to lie.

It’s pretty nice that “The Wailing” doesn’t suffer from this problem. I think the scene that did it for me would be when the shaman was performing the ritual to exorcise evil spirit from the officer’s daughter. She was convulsing, clearly in an enormous amount of pain, with her situation getting worse as the ritual continued. Eventually the officer breaks and interrupts the ritual, stopping his daughters’ pain. It might not make sense to us as third party viewers with no serious attachment to the well being of the daughter, but I think this, for me, was what humanized the father. It showed a man desperate to stop his daughter from pain and suffering, even at the cost stopping the ritual.

However, I will note that some of the characters’ propensity towards silence/witholding information is kinda self-frustrating (looking at you old man and lady in white).

### The ending brings more questions than the beginning

To be honest, a decent amount of horror movies do have ambiguous endings, but very few can match the quality of this movie. The things I knew for certain was that the entire family (except the daughter I think) was dead, the old man was the devil, the shaman was currently in cahoots with the devil, and the lady in white was good. 

With these facts in mind, I quickly took to the internet in search of explanations to the ending of the movie. From what I saw, there are two compelling interpretations as to what exactly happened.

1. **Lady Good, Shaman + Old Man Bad**: This is supported by the fact that the old man turns into the devil, and the shaman tries to misdirect the officer’s suspicions onto the lady. However, this doesn’t fully explain why the shaman tries to kill the old man.
2. **Lady Bad, Shaman + Old Man Good**: This could make sense because the old man might have taken photos/momentos of the victims in an attempt to perform some sort of spiritual resistance. This would mean that the shaman’s mistake was genuine, and his ritual to kill the old man was successful (at least enough to weaken him to the point where the lady was able to control his body). Then this might be enough to explain why he turns into a devil. I’m more inclined to believe this theory just because the old man actions don’t really seem that malevolent until later in the story. 

Regardless of who’s good and evil, the one thing I’ve truly come to appreciate about “The Wailing” is that it left me grappling with the events that occured in the movie long after it’s conclusion. The more you think about it, the better the film becomes. Easily a 9.5/10 horror movie.
